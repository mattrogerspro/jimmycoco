# CTA policy

An education programme that sells too early stops being an education programme,
and the whole strategy depends on being the source people trust. But content
that never routes anywhere is a cost centre. The policy below is how both stay
true.

## The principle

**The CTA is the next useful thing, not the next commercial thing.**

Every article has an obvious next action from the reader's point of view. If
she's just worked out what a tan costs her, the next useful thing is a
calculator where she can put her own numbers in — not a product page. The
product page is two steps later, and she'll get there on her own if the model
told her the truth.

## One primary CTA per article

Named in `brief.md` before writing. Placed **after** the article has delivered
its answer — never in the first third, never interrupting the model.

Routes, in order of how often they should be used:

| Route | Use when | Looks like |
|---|---|---|
| **A tool** | The article contains a model the reader will want to run on her own numbers | "Run your own figures in the profit calculator" |
| **Another article** | The piece raises a question it doesn't answer | "What to charge for a spray tan in 2026" |
| **A template** | The article describes a document she should have | "Download the consultation and consent pack" |
| **The Salon Business Brief** | Nothing more specific fits | One line, no pop-up, no interstitial |
| **The litre / trade account** | The article's honest conclusion actually points at the product | "Order the litre — £60" |

## Secondary CTA

At most one, at the foot. Usually the newsletter if the primary was a tool, or a
tool if the primary was the newsletter.

## Hard rules

- **No gating.** Tools and templates are open, per
  [decision 3](../00-strategy/decisions.md#3-tools-and-templates-ungated-all-of-them).
  No email wall on anything the article promised.
- **No pop-ups, no interstitials, no exit-intent, no sticky bars** on article
  pages. They damage the thing we're building and Google treats intrusive
  interstitials as a ranking problem.
- **No CTA above the answer.** The reader gets what she came for first.
- **Never bend a fact to reach a CTA.** If the honest conclusion doesn't point
  at the product, the CTA is a tool or another article. The costing pillar
  concludes that solution price is the smallest lever in the model — that
  conclusion publishes unchanged, and it is a better argument for buying a good
  litre than any claim we could make.
- **CTA labels are specific.** "Order the litre — £60", "Run your own figures".
  Never "Learn more" or "Discover".
- **Compliance articles do not sell.** Pillar 2 pieces route to templates,
  other articles, or the Brief. Never to a product. Trading on a licensing
  worry is exactly the fear-lever the voice rules prohibit, and it would cost us
  the authority the pillar exists to build.

## Product mentions inside the body

Allowed, and often more honest than the alternative — the £60 litre in a worked
example is the litre we sell, and an anonymous "a typical litre" is a pretence.

Conditions: name it plainly, use the real current price, and only where it is
genuinely the natural example. Never more than twice in a body. Never as the
answer to a question it isn't the answer to.

## The Salon Business Brief

The standing newsletter CTA. One line, in the flow of the page, at the foot:

> **The Salon Business Brief** — one email a month, one number worth knowing.
> [Subscribe]

No incentive, no lead magnet, no "join 5,000 salon owners" until that's true.
