Pricing Architecture Review

Sunless by Jimmy Coco · Prepared 5 August 2026 · 23 active UK products · Trailing 12 months of sales

Summary

The range is priced with a consistent premium logic — 87% of products end in a round number — but three structural problems undermine it. The bestselling product is sold at two different prices simultaneously, five separate products share the single price point of £22, and 83% of the range sits on a permanent discount, which has removed any meaning from the word "sale".

The most valuable finding is not a psychological one. It is that the product generating 64% of all revenue is listed twice, at £22 and at £18, with the same SKU and the same barcode.

▲ Critical — same SKU, same barcode, £4 apart

6,036 orders paid £18 for what 5,706 orders paid £22. Which price a customer pays depends entirely on which listing they happen to land on. Closing the gap upward would be worth up to £24,100 a year — though that figure assumes no volume is lost, which is unlikely, and the real answer depends on which price the market will bear.

◆ Why this matters more than the psychology

No pricing strategy can be evaluated while the same product carries two prices. Every elasticity signal in the data is contaminated: the business cannot tell whether £22 is too high, because a third of buyers are being shown £18 for the identical item. This should be resolved before any other pricing change is considered.

2. Is £22 a "nowhere price"?

Broadly, yes — though for a more specific reason than price endings.

£20 is a genuine psychological ceiling. Shoppers store prices by their leading digit, so £19.99 is filed as "nineteen-something" and £22 as "twenty-something". At £22 the business has crossed that barrier and paid the perceptual cost of doing so, but gained only £2 for it. It is neither the accessible impulse price that sits under £20, nor a price high enough to signal a different tier of product.

.801Reads as a calculation, not a price — Head-to-Toe Glow at £28.80

The round-number convention is a real asset: it is the pricing language of premium beauty and it is applied consistently across almost the whole range. The three exceptions dilute it.

£28.80 is the most conspicuous. It is exactly 20% off £36.00 — a discount calculation that has been saved as a permanent price. It looks like a system output rather than a decision, and it sits awkwardly beside the £28.00 Malibu Beach Duo, where a shopper sees an 80p difference and no reason for it.

4. Permanent discounting has neutralised the sale signal

▲ High — the word "sale" no longer does any work

19 of 23 products (83%) carry a "was" price, at a median discount of 20% and a range of 15–25%. When almost everything is always reduced, a reduction stops being a reason to act.

There are two costs. Commercially, the business has no lever left to pull for a genuine promotional moment — a flash sale has to discount from an already-discounted price to feel like anything. Reputationally, a reference price that is never actually charged is not a legitimate "was" price under UK pricing rules, which expect the higher price to have been the genuine selling price for a meaningful period.

Four products carry no reference price at all — including the £22 soufflé and the £39 Self-Tan Edit. The bestselling product in the range is the one with no saving shown, while its own duplicate advertises "was £22".

5. The hero product is priced below its own accessories bundle

Average order value is £26.93. The soufflé — the product driving 64% of revenue — sits at £22, below that average. Meanwhile the £24 Malibu Kit includes that same soufflé plus three accessories for £2 more.

A customer choosing between a £22 soufflé and a £24 four-piece kit has an easy decision, and it is not the one that serves the business. The kit is doing the work of a discount rather than an upsell, and it is dragging the hero product's perceived value down with it.

Recommendations

  1. Resolve the duplicate soufflé listing first Merge the two listings, or set both to the same price. Nothing else in this review can be acted on with confidence until one product has one price. This is the single highest-value change available.
  2. Break up the £22 cluster Five products at one price point wastes the range's ability to signal tier. Move the soufflé to its own level — £25 is the strongest candidate — and space the bundles at £24, £29 and £34 so each reads as a distinct step.
  3. Commit to one price-ending language The round-number convention already covers 87% of the range and suits the brand. Bring the three outliers into line: £9.99 → £10, £12.50 → £12, £28.80 → £29.
  4. Cut the number of products on permanent discount Reduce from 83% to roughly a third. Let the rest sit at full price so that a genuine promotion has somewhere to fall from. This also removes the "was" price exposure.
  5. Give the soufflé a reference price The bestseller currently shows no saving at all. Once the duplicate is resolved, a legitimate compare-at price is the easiest available conversion gain.
  6. Fill the £39–£59 gap There is nothing between the Self-Tan Edit and the A-List Glow Kit. A £45–£49 kit would give customers a step up from £39 rather than a £20 jump, and would lift average order value.
  7. Test before committing on the hero product The soufflé carries 64% of revenue. Any change to it should be tested on one shade or one channel first — the downside of getting it wrong is larger than the upside of getting it right quickly.

Method & scope. All 23 active, published UK products were retrieved from the Shopify Admin API with live prices, compare-at prices and inventory. The duplicate soufflé listing was confirmed by matching SKU (IM4, IM5) and barcode (0653748599864) across both product records, not by title similarity. Revenue and order figures are drawn from Shopify sales analytics for the trailing twelve months to 5 August 2026. The £24,100 figure represents the arithmetic upper bound of closing the price gap upward and assumes no volume is lost; it is illustrative of scale, not a forecast. Recommendations on price levels are directional — none can be validated without cost-of-goods data and live elasticity testing. No changes were made to the store during this review.
