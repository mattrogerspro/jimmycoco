# Voice — for education content

The brand voice is *quietly authoritative, warm, editorial, reassuring,
precise; never discount-led, never inflated beauty clichés*. UK spelling
throughout — colour, personalise, organisation, licence (noun) / license (verb).

Education content is that same voice pointed at a different job. Marketing copy
makes someone want something. This makes someone able to do something. The
adjustments below are what changes.

## Read like a trusted supplier, not a magazine

The reader is a business owner reading between clients. She is not being
seduced; she's checking something.

- **Answer first, then explain.** The number goes in the first hundred words. If
  she stops reading after the summary box, she should still have got what she
  came for.
- **Plain sentences.** Editorial in taste, not in ornament. No scene-setting
  opening paragraph. No "In today's competitive beauty landscape".
- **Second person.** "What it costs you", not "what it costs the salon owner".
- **Show the arithmetic.** £60 ÷ 28 = £2.14. Never present a derived figure
  without the working, because the working is why she'll trust the next one.

## Confidence is calibrated, not performed

This is the part that distinguishes us. Three registers, and using the wrong one
is the most damaging error possible here:

| When | Register | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Sourced fact | State it plainly, cite it | "Tanning has the highest no-show rate of any treatment category, at 3.14%." |
| Genuine professional disagreement | Present both, name who holds each | "Trainers disagree. goGLOW put violet bases on cool skin tones; Sjolie recommends them for warm. There is no study either way." |
| No evidence exists | Say so, and say what follows | "There is no published ventilation standard for spray tan booths. Anyone quoting an air-changes-per-hour figure is quoting a preference, not a requirement." |

Saying "nobody knows" is a feature. It is the single most credible thing we can
write, because no competitor does it.

## Never

- **A number without a source or stated working.** This is the hard rule.
- **A number in dollars**, or a US figure presented as though it applies here.
- **Anything on the [claims register](../03-research/claims-register.md)**
  asserted as fact.
- **Fake specificity.** "Most salons" when we mean some. "Studies show" without
  the study. Precision we haven't earned reads worse than an honest range.
- **Fear as a lever.** Compliance content especially. Not "you could be fined
  £5,000" — instead, what the rule is, where it comes from, what to do.
- **Testimonials, ratings or review counts** unless they are real, verifiable
  and attributable. This has burned the site once already.
- **Discount language, urgency, or exclamation marks.**

## Talking about ourselves

We sell things to the reader, and pretending otherwise is worse than being
straightforward about it.

- Use our own product in worked examples where it's the honest example — the £60
  litre is the litre we sell, and saying so is more credible than an anonymous
  "a typical litre".
- Never make our product the answer to a question it isn't the answer to. The
  costing article's honest conclusion is that solution price is the *smallest*
  lever in the model. Publish that. It's true, it's useful, and it's a better
  argument for a good litre than any claim we could make about the litre.
- Competitor solutions are referred to neutrally or not at all. No brand
  comparisons on quality. Comparisons on published, checkable facts only.

## Register examples

**Wrong** — "Getting your pricing right is one of the most important things you
can do for your salon business! Let's take a look at some of the key factors."

**Right** — "At £30 a tan, the solution in the bottle costs you £2.14. That's 7%
of the ticket. Almost everything you're worried about when you compare litre
prices is happening inside those 7 pence in the pound — and almost none of your
margin is."
